The priceof fog.

Tuition climbed as the real value of a new graduate’s first pay fell. The working paper names this collision the Double Inflation Principle.

Between academic years 2010–11 and 2024–25, the paper reports a 3.89× rise in its private-college fee index, while its national series shows real entry pay falling to 69.7. Its central argument: as a degree becomes a weaker signal, families are pushed into a more expensive contest for position.

02 / THE MECHANISM THE PAPER PROPOSES

A positional
arms race.

The Double Inflation Principle is the paper’s name for a feedback loop—not simply “fees up, wages down.” It argues that when a degree loses some of its signalling power, households may respond by buying a stronger, costlier signal.

01  /  THE CREDENTIAL

More degrees, less distinction.

As higher education expands, a degree alone may do less to set one applicant apart. The paper frames this as credential devaluation.

↘
02  /  THE RESPONSE

Households move upmarket.

Students may pursue another qualification or a higher-priced college to signal quality. The paper describes this demand-side leg as partial evidence, not a settled result.

↗
03  /  THE PRICE

Position gets more expensive.

Upmarket demand can give high-status providers room to price beyond rising costs—deepening the gap between what a credential costs and what it returns.

DATA NOTE / SERIES

Not every line has the same status.

Published annual series: the education CPI and real entry-pay values plotted here are taken from Table 1 of the working paper and rebased to 2010–11 = 100.

Reported endpoints: the paper reports the DIP-50 fee index at 100 → 389 and the premium-strategy value-per-rupee index at 100 → 62.9. Since annual paths for those measures were not provided as observations in the supplied files, their in-between values are shown only as simple bridges.

Different segments differ: the paper’s Table 5 reports regional and volume strategies above their 2010 baseline at the end of the period, while the premium strategy is at 62.9. The premium result should not be generalized to every degree or college.

READ BEFORE SHARING / LIMITS

This is a working paper, not a verdict.

The PDF identifies itself as an independent working paper prepared for journal submission. It is not presented here as peer-reviewed research.

The manuscript says parts of the college-level historical fee, salary and placement panel are reconstructed, and distinguishes those paths from published data. It also characterizes the upmarket-demand evidence as partial and notes that this test relies on a small set of published fee histories.

This page is a visual explainer of the supplied manuscript—not an independent replication or audit. Consult the paper for definitions, sources, model details and its full limitations.
